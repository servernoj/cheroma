RC discharge: identify \(V_0\) and \(\tau\) from \((t, V)\) data

Step-by-step guide for MATLAB. You enter ts and vs and run the steps yourself. Math below uses LaTeX (MathJax). No noise is assumed in your synthetic experiment.

Model and residual

Time constant \(\tau = RC\). Ideal capacitor discharge:

$$V(t) = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}$$

Parameter vector (column \(2 \times 1\)):

$$\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} V_0 \\ \tau \end{pmatrix}$$

For each sample \(k = 1,\ldots,N\), with measurements \((t_k, V^{\mathrm{meas}}_k)\) stored as ts(k), vs(k):

$$r_k(\mathbf{p}) = V^{\mathrm{meas}}_k - V_0\, e^{-t_k/\tau}$$

Stack \(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p}) = [r_1,\ldots,r_N]^\top\). Objective (half sum of squares):

$$\Phi(\mathbf{p}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{r}\|_2^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^N r_k^2$$

Stage 0 — Data in MATLAB and a plot

You do:

  1. Define column vectors ts and vs.
  2. plot(ts, vs, 'o'); label axes.
  3. Check numel(ts) == numel(vs).

Why: If \(t=0\) is the start of discharge, \(V_0\) is the model value at \(t=0\) (possibly extrapolated if your first sample has \(t_1 > 0\)).

Stage 1 — Residual function \(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})\)

You do: Implement r = residual_rc(p, ts, vs) with p(1)=V_0, p(2)=tau:

function r = residual_rc(p, ts, vs)
  V0 = p(1);
  tau = p(2);
  vhat = V0 * exp(-ts / tau);
  r = vs - vhat;
end

Pick a trial \(\mathbf{p}_0 = [V_{0,\mathrm{guess}};\,\tau_{\mathrm{guess}}]^\top\). Compute r0 = residual_rc(p0, ts, vs) and

$$\Phi(\mathbf{p}_0) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{r}_0^\top \mathbf{r}_0$$

Stage 2a — Numerical Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}\)

Dimensions: \(\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}\), with \(J_{kj} = \partial r_k / \partial p_j\).

Meaning of \(\mathbf{e}_1\), \(\mathbf{e}_2\), \(h_1\), \(h_2\).

\(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\) are the standard basis vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (you have two parameters):

$$\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

So \(\mathbf{p} + h_1 \mathbf{e}_1\) bumps only \(V_0\) by \(h_1\), and \(\mathbf{p} + h_2 \mathbf{e}_2\) bumps only \(\tau\) by \(h_2\). Each column of \(\mathbf{J}\) is the rate of change of \(\mathbf{r}\) along one coordinate of \(\mathbf{p}\).

\(h_1\) and \(h_2\) are small finite-difference step sizes for those two coordinates. They may differ because \(V_0\) and \(\tau\) can live on very different scales (e.g. volts vs seconds). A common rule is something like \(h_j \sim 10^{-6}\max(1, |p_j|)\), not necessarily one shared \(h\) for both.

In each case you approximate a partial derivative:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial p_j} \approx \frac{\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p} + h_j \mathbf{e}_j) - \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})}{h_j}$$

Forward differences (you choose small \(h_1, h_2\)):

$$\mathbf{J}_{:1} \approx \frac{\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p} + h_1 \mathbf{e}_1) - \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})}{h_1}, \qquad \mathbf{J}_{:2} \approx \frac{\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p} + h_2 \mathbf{e}_2) - \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})}{h_2}$$

with \(\mathbf{e}_1 = [1,0]^\top\), \(\mathbf{e}_2 = [0,1]^\top\). In MATLAB: e1 = [1;0]; e2 = [0;1]; then build J(:,1), J(:,2); confirm size(J) == [N,2].

Stage 3 — Gauss–Newton step

Normal equations for the Gauss–Newton increment \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\):

$$\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J}\,\Delta\mathbf{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r}$$

Then \(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{new}} = \mathbf{p} + \Delta\mathbf{p}\).

A = J' * J;
b = -(J' * r0);
dp = A \ b;
p1 = p0 + dp;

Recompute \(\mathbf{r}\) at \(\mathbf{p}_1\); compare \(\Phi\) before and after. Loop until \(\|\Delta\mathbf{p}\|\) is small.

Stage 2b — Analytical Jacobian

Predicted voltage \(\hat{V}(t) = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}\). Since \(r_k = V^{\mathrm{meas}}_k - \hat{V}(t_k)\),

$$\frac{\partial r_k}{\partial V_0} = -\frac{\partial \hat{V}}{\partial V_0}, \qquad \frac{\partial r_k}{\partial \tau} = -\frac{\partial \hat{V}}{\partial \tau}$$

Derivatives of \(\hat{V}(t) = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}\):

$$\frac{\partial \hat{V}}{\partial V_0} = e^{-t/\tau}$$

Using \(\dfrac{d}{d\tau} e^{-t/\tau} = e^{-t/\tau}\cdot \dfrac{t}{\tau^2}\):

$$\frac{\partial \hat{V}}{\partial \tau} = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}\,\frac{t}{\tau^2}$$

So (column-wise, evaluated at each ts(k)):

$$\mathbf{J}_{:1} = -\exp(-\mathbf{t}/\tau), \qquad \mathbf{J}_{:2} = -V_0\, \exp(-\mathbf{t}/\tau) \odot (\mathbf{t}/\tau^2)$$

where \(\mathbf{t}\) is the vector ts and \(\odot\) is element-wise product (use .* in MATLAB).

You do: J = jacobian_rc_analytic(p, ts) returning N×2; repeat Stage 3.

Stage 4 — Optional: Levenberg–Marquardt

What is \(\Phi\)? The scalar objective you minimize is the half sum of squared residuals (same \(\Phi\) as in the model section):

$$\Phi(\mathbf{p}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})\|_2^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_k r_k^2.$$

So “\(\Phi\) decreased” means the total squared error decreased. Each GN/LM step tries to choose \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) so that \(\Phi(\mathbf{p}+\Delta\mathbf{p})\) is smaller than \(\Phi(\mathbf{p})\), using a linear approximation of \(\mathbf{r}\) near \(\mathbf{p}\).

What is \(\lambda\)? A non‑negative damping parameter (Levenberg–Marquardt). It does not come from the RC physics; it is only a numerical knob for the solver. Roughly:

  • \(\lambda\) small → the step is close to Gauss–Newton (uses \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\) aggressively).
  • \(\lambda\) large → the step is smaller and behaves more like a short move along the negative gradient of \(\Phi\) (since \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r} = \nabla\Phi\)), i.e. more cautious.

In practice: increase \(\lambda\) when a proposed step would increase \(\Phi\) or is unstable; decrease \(\lambda\) when steps are accepted and convergence is healthy.

Why add \(\lambda \mathbf{I}\) to \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\)? The Gauss–Newton system is \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\,\Delta\mathbf{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}\). Two common failures:

  1. Singular or ill‑conditioned \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\): the solve for \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) is badly posed; \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) can be huge or hypersensitive to tiny errors.
  2. Linearization poor far from the minimum: a full GN step can overshoot, so \(\Phi(\mathbf{p}+\Delta\mathbf{p}) > \Phi(\mathbf{p})\).

Replacing \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\) by \(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \lambda \mathbf{I}\) gives:

$$(\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{J} + \lambda \mathbf{I})\,\Delta\mathbf{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top \mathbf{r}$$

Effects: (i) With \(\lambda > 0\), the matrix is better conditioned (strictly positive definite in the usual case), so the linear system for \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) is more stable and wild steps from near‑singularity are curbed. (ii) Increasing \(\lambda\) shrinks \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\), trading GN aggressiveness for robustness when the quadratic model of \(\Phi\) is unreliable.

One‑line intuition: GN trusts the linearization fully; LM adds “\(\lambda \mathbf{I}\)” to damp the step—if the full GN move is bad, blend in a shorter, gradient‑like correction.

You do: Implement the same solve with A = J'*J + lambda*eye(2), tune \(\lambda\) by hand, or mirror the accept/reject schedule used in your JS fitter.

Stage 5 — MATLAB lsqnonlin (check)

lsqnonlin(@(p) residual_rc(p, ts, vs), p0, ...) and compare to your GN iterations.

Stage 6 — Optional: log-domain line fit (warm start only)

If \(V_k > 0\) for all \(k\), then \(\ln V_k = \ln V_0 - t_k/\tau\) is affine in \(t_k\). Linear regression on \((t_k, \ln V_k)\) gives rough \((\ln V_0,\, -1/\tau)\) — useful for \(\mathbf{p}_0\), not a replacement for nonlinear least squares when noise or model mismatch matter.

Appendix — Why \(\Phi\) decreases: gradient and descent direction

This section generalizes beyond the RC example. Read the formulas here with MathJax rendering enabled.

Objective and gradient. With \(\Phi(\mathbf{p}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})\|_2^2\), one has (chain rule):

$$\nabla \Phi(\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{p})^\top \mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p})$$

The gradient is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^M\). It points in the direction of steepest increase of \(\Phi\). At an unconstrained minimizer, \(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p}^\ast)=\mathbf{0}\).

It is not correct to say the gradient is “negative” as a whole: components can have mixed signs, and the direction depends on where \(\mathbf{p}\) lies.

Directional derivative along a step \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\). The rate of change of \(\Phi\) as you move from \(\mathbf{p}\) in direction \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) is

$$\left.\frac{d}{d\alpha}\Phi(\mathbf{p}+\alpha\Delta\mathbf{p})\right|_{\alpha=0} = \nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p})^\top \Delta\mathbf{p}$$

A descent direction satisfies \(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{p})^\top \Delta\mathbf{p} < 0\): then for sufficiently small step length, \(\Phi\) decreases. That is the precise meaning of “\(\Phi\) goes down because its rate of change along the update is negative.”

Gauss–Newton chooses \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) from the linearized model so that (under standard assumptions) this inner product is negative — a descent step. Levenberg–Marquardt shrinks \(\Delta\mathbf{p}\) when needed so that an accepted step still reduces \(\Phi\) (or increases \(\lambda\) until it does).

Linear-in-parameters models (strong case). If \(\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{p}) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{b}\) (affine in \(\mathbf{p}\)), then \(\Phi(\mathbf{p})\) is a convex quadratic. Gauss–Newton coincides with minimizing that quadratic exactly: one solve reaches the global minimizer (in exact arithmetic). Example: scalar \(y_i \approx k x_i\) gives \(\Phi(k)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i (y_i-kx_i)^2\), a parabola in \(k\).

Nonlinear \(\mathbf{r}\) (typical calibration). Then \(\Phi\) is not a global quadratic. There is no universal guarantee that every raw GN step reduces \(\Phi\); trust-region / LM damping and step acceptance rules are what enforce practical decrease. Convergence is usually local (good initial guess, full-rank Jacobian), not “from any start to a global minimum.”

Also: \(\Phi\to 0\) only if the model can fit all data exactly (\(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}\)); with noise or model error, the minimum \(\Phi\) is typically positive.
