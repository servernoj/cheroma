Compute \(\hat u,\hat v\) in the world frame (general case)

Goal: build two unit vectors \(\hat u,\hat v\) spanning the plane perpendicular to the spinner axis \(\hat t\), and express all three (\(\hat u,\hat v,\hat t\)) in the world/base frame.

Step 1 — compute the spinner axis direction \(\hat t\) in world

The spinner axis is along the tool/wrist centerline. In the general (non-\(\gamma=\pi\)) case, let:

\[ \gamma = q_1+q_2+q_3 \]

Why \(\hat t = [\sin\gamma\cos q_0,\; \sin\gamma\sin q_0,\; \cos\gamma]^T\)

This comes from building the direction in the arm’s vertical plane and then rotating it around \(Z\) by the base yaw \(q_0\).

1a) Direction in the arm plane \((r,z)\)

In your convention, \(\gamma\) is measured from \(+Z\) toward the horizontal reach direction \(+r\). A unit vector in that plane has components:

\[ r = \sin\gamma,\qquad z = \cos\gamma \]

(Checks: \(\gamma=0\Rightarrow[r,z]=[0,1]\) up; \(\gamma=90^\circ\Rightarrow[1,0]\) horizontal; \(\gamma=180^\circ\Rightarrow[0,-1]\) down.)

1b) Rotate the horizontal part into world \(x,y\) using yaw \(q_0\)

The planar “horizontal magnitude” is \(r=\sin\gamma\). Base yaw \(q_0\) rotates that horizontal direction around \(Z\), so:

\[ x = (\sin\gamma)\cos q_0,\qquad y = (\sin\gamma)\sin q_0 \]

The vertical component is unchanged by yaw:

\[ z = \cos\gamma \]

Therefore the unit tool-axis/spinner-axis direction in world coordinates is:

\[ \hat t = \begin{bmatrix} \sin\gamma\cos q_0\\ \sin\gamma\sin q_0\\ \cos\gamma \end{bmatrix} \]

Use radians inside \(\sin(\cdot),\cos(\cdot)\).

Step 2 — choose an in-plane reference direction \(\hat v\) in world

To define the \((\hat u,\hat v)\) basis, you need one additional direction that you can describe in world coordinates. A practical choice is a direction that lies in the bracket face, such as the wrist pitch axis direction.

If you want \(\hat v\) to represent the pitch-axis direction, then in the world frame it rotates with the base yaw \(q_0\). Let \(\hat v_0=[0,1,0]^T\) be the pitch axis direction when \(q_0=0\). After yaw:

\[ \hat v(q_0)=R_z(q_0)\hat v_0= \begin{bmatrix} -\sin q_0\\ \cos q_0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

This \(\hat v(q_0)\) is the same physical axis direction, expressed in world coordinates.

\(\hat v\) must not be parallel to \(\hat t\). If they become nearly parallel, the cross product will be near zero and cannot be normalized.

Step 3 — build \(\hat u\) from a cross product, then re-orthogonalize

Compute:

\[ \hat u = \frac{\hat v \times \hat t}{\|\hat v \times \hat t\|} \]

In the common case where you use: \[ \hat t= \begin{bmatrix} \sin\gamma\cos q_0\\ \sin\gamma\sin q_0\\ \cos\gamma \end{bmatrix}, \quad \hat v(q_0)= \begin{bmatrix} -\sin q_0\\ \cos q_0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \] the cross product simplifies to:

\[ \hat u = \hat v\times \hat t = \begin{bmatrix} \cos q_0\cos\gamma\\ \sin q_0\cos\gamma\\ -\sin\gamma \end{bmatrix} \]

(This vector already has unit length, so the normalization step does not change it, assuming exact math.)

Component formula for the cross product (general)

If you have arbitrary vectors:

\[ \mathbf{t}=\begin{bmatrix}t_x\\t_y\\t_z\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}v_x\\v_y\\v_z\end{bmatrix} \]

and you define:

\[ \mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{t} \]

then the components are:

\[ \mathbf{u}= \begin{bmatrix} u_x\\u_y\\u_z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_y t_z - v_z t_y\\ v_z t_x - v_x t_z\\ v_x t_y - v_y t_x \end{bmatrix} \]

To make it a unit vector:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|},\qquad \|\mathbf{u}\|=\sqrt{u_x^2+u_y^2+u_z^2} \]

Order matters: \(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{v}=-(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{t})\).

Where does \(u_x=v_y t_z - v_z t_y\) come from?

A compact definition of the cross product uses a determinant:

\[ \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{t}= \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}}\\ v_x & v_y & v_z\\ t_x & t_y & t_z \end{vmatrix} \]

Expanding this determinant along the first row gives:

\[ \mathbf{u} = \hat{\mathbf{i}} \begin{vmatrix} v_y & v_z\\ t_y & t_z \end{vmatrix} - \hat{\mathbf{j}} \begin{vmatrix} v_x & v_z\\ t_x & t_z \end{vmatrix} + \hat{\mathbf{k}} \begin{vmatrix} v_x & v_y\\ t_x & t_y \end{vmatrix} \]

Therefore the \(x\)-component is the coefficient of \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\):

\[ u_x= \begin{vmatrix} v_y & v_z\\ t_y & t_z \end{vmatrix} = v_y t_z - v_z t_y \]

Intuition: \(u_x\) depends only on the \(y,z\) components because it is the signed “area term” from projecting both vectors into the \(yz\) plane.

Then recompute \(\hat v\) so the basis is orthonormal:

\[ \hat v \leftarrow \hat t \times \hat u \]

Now \((\hat u,\hat v,\hat t)\) are:

  • unit length,
  • mutually perpendicular,
  • right-handed (because \(\hat u \times \hat v = \hat t\)).
