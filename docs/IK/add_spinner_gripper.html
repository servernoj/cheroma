Add spinner + gripper: what changes in FK/IK

You currently model a 4-DOF arm (base yaw + shoulder/elbow/wrist pitch) with a fixed TCP at the end of \(L_3\). Adding the last two DOFs (wrist spinner/roll + gripper open/close) is easy if the spinner axis is centered on the TCP, but becomes more interesting if the spinner axis is offset and the gripper is eccentric around that axis.

1) What you are adding

  • Spinner (roll) joint: an additional revolute joint \(q_4\) whose axis is parallel to the wrist centerline / tool axis (i.e., perpendicular to the wrist pitch axis).
  • Gripper: an actuator \(q_5\) (open/close). Kinematically it is usually not a rotation of the arm chain; it changes grasp width, not pose.
  • Offsets:
    • Spinner axis is translated from the wrist pitch frame by a fixed vector (offset in two coordinates in the wrist/tool frame).
    • TCP/gripper mounting point is not centered on the spinner axis, so rotating \(q_4\) moves the TCP on a circle of radius \(R\) in the plane perpendicular to the spinner axis.
The key complication is: roll can affect position if the TCP is eccentric about the roll axis. If the TCP were centered, roll would only affect orientation.

2) How FK changes

Conceptually, keep your existing FK up to the wrist pitch frame, then append two rigid transforms and one roll rotation.

Most practical viewpoint (matches your current repo code)

In your current implementation, apps/api/src/modules/kinematics.js implements:

  • \(FK(q_0,q_1,q_2)\rightarrow (x,y,z)\), and this output is the position of the free end of \(L_3\).
  • This FK is already specialized to the \(\gamma=\pi\) case (tool axis assumed “down”), so \(q_3\) does not affect position there.

To add spinner+gripper without drowning in frames, treat your current FK output as a reference point on the wrist centerline:

\[ P_{ref} = [x,y,z]^T \]

Then compute the TCP (the chess piece centerline when grasped) by adding:

  • a fixed spinner axis offset (2 parameters) perpendicular to the tool axis, and
  • a roll-dependent eccentric offset of radius \(R\) from the spinner axis to the piece centerline.

Step A — tool axis direction \(\hat{t}\)

The spinner axis is parallel to the tool axis (wrist centerline). You need a unit direction \(\hat{t}\) in world coordinates.

  • If you keep \(\gamma=\pi\) during motion (as your current FK assumes), the tool axis is simply: \[ \hat{t} = [0,0,-1]^T \]
  • If later you use a general FK with \(\gamma=q_1+q_2+q_3\), then: \[ \hat{t} = \begin{bmatrix} \sin\gamma \cos q_0\\ \sin\gamma \sin q_0\\ \cos\gamma \end{bmatrix} \] (angles in radians inside \(\sin,\cos\)).

Step B — define a perpendicular basis \((\hat{u},\hat{v})\)

The spinner axis offset is 2D: it lives in the plane perpendicular to \(\hat{t}\). Build two perpendicular unit vectors spanning that plane:

To make \((\hat u,\hat v)\) meaningful on your hardware, choose \(\hat v\) to align with a repeatable direction on the wrist/bracket. A very practical choice is the pitch-axis direction (the direction of the wrist pitch axis), expressed in world coordinates.

In your kinematic convention, the pitch-axis direction when \(q_0=0\) is \(\hat v_0=[0,1,0]^T\). After base yaw \(q_0\), the same physical axis expressed in world coordinates is:

\[ \hat v(q_0)= \begin{bmatrix} -\sin q_0\\ \cos q_0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Now build \(\hat u\) by the right-hand rule and normalize:

\[ \hat u = \frac{\hat v \times \hat t}{\|\hat v \times \hat t\|} \]

Then recompute \(\hat v\) so the basis is orthonormal:

\[ \hat v \leftarrow \hat t \times \hat u \]

With \(\hat t=[\sin\gamma\cos q_0,\sin\gamma\sin q_0,\cos\gamma]^T\) and \(\hat v(q_0)\) as above, the cross product simplifies to a closed form: \[ \hat u = \begin{bmatrix} \cos q_0\cos\gamma\\ \sin q_0\cos\gamma\\ -\sin\gamma \end{bmatrix} \] (unit length in exact math).

Step C — spinner axis location (2 parameters)

Let \((d_u,d_v)\) be the constant offset (mm) from the wrist centerline at \(P_{ref}\) to the spinner axis, expressed in the \((\hat{u},\hat{v})\) basis:

\[ P_{spin} = P_{ref} + d_u \hat{u} + d_v \hat{v} \]

How to measure \((d_u,d_v)\) in practice

  1. Move the arm to a pose where the tool axis is approximately vertical (easy to reason about).
  2. Identify the wrist centerline and the spinner axis centerline on the hardware (use calipers; or mark their projections on the table if you can).
  3. Measure the signed lateral offsets between those centerlines (mm). Those are your \((d_u,d_v)\).
If your tool axis is vertical during measurement, then \((d_u,d_v)\) are literally “X and Y offsets” on the table plane.

Step D — eccentric offset to the chess-piece centerline (radius \(R\))

Let \(R\) be the distance (mm) from the spinner axis to the TCP centerline (the chess piece centerline while grasped). Let \(\phi_0\) be the “zero-roll” angle defining where the TCP sits around the spinner axis when \(q_4=0\).

Then the roll-dependent offset is:

\[ \Delta P_{ecc}(q_4) = R\Big(\cos(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{u} + \sin(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{v}\Big) \]

and the final TCP position is:

\[ P_{TCP} = P_{spin} + \Delta P_{ecc}(q_4) + L_{axis}\,\hat{t} \]

Expanding \(P_{spin}\) and \(\Delta P_{ecc}\), this can be written as one expression:

\[ P_{TCP} = P_{ref} + d_u \hat{u} + d_v \hat{v} + R\Big(\cos(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{u} + \sin(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{v}\Big) + L_{axis}\,\hat{t} \]

where \(L_{axis}\) is an optional constant shift along the tool axis (mm) if your “piece centerline point” is not in the same cross-section as \(P_{ref}\). If your current FK’s \(P_{ref}\) already corresponds to the height you care about, set \(L_{axis}=0\).

How to measure \(R\) (and \(\phi_0\))

  1. Close the gripper on a representative chess piece and decide what point you consider the TCP (usually the piece centerline).
  2. Measure the perpendicular distance from the spinner axis centerline to that piece centerline (calipers). That is \(R\).
  3. Define your roll zero \(q_4=0\) (e.g., jaws parallel to board files). Set \(\phi_0=0\) by definition in that configuration; otherwise measure \(\phi_0\).
If the gripper is not symmetric, the “piece centerline” may shift slightly with gripper opening \(q_5\). Start by treating \(R\) as constant for your typical grasp width; refine later with \(R(q_5)\) if needed.

3) How to implement IK (clear path)

Your current IK already uses a hybrid strategy: an analytical initial guess (assuming \(dX=0\)) followed by a Newton solve that accounts for \(dX\). The spinner+gripper extension fits the same pattern: keep an analytical stage to generate a good initial guess, then refine numerically for the full model.

Stage 0 — define what IK should solve

  • Inputs: desired TCP position \(P^{des}=[x,y,z]^T\) (world) and desired tool pitch \(\gamma_{des}\) (often \(\pi\) for “down”).
  • Optional: desired roll \(q_4\) (jaw alignment), and gripper command \(q_5\) (open/close).
  • Outputs: joint angles \(q_0..q_3\) (and optionally \(q_4,q_5\)).

Stage 1 — choose \(q_4\) (roll) and \(q_5\) (gripper)

For chess, it’s common to choose roll from task logic (e.g. align jaws with files/ranks) and treat \(q_5\) as purely task-level. If you choose \(q_4\) first, the remaining IK becomes “4-DOF position+pitch” with a known roll-dependent TCP offset.

Common chess heuristic (when the tool axis is close to vertical): set spinner roll to cancel base yaw so the jaws remain parallel to the board files: \[ q_4 \approx -q_0 + q_{4,0} \] where \(q_{4,0}\) is your “jaws parallel to files at \(q_0=0\)” constant. If the tool axis tilts significantly, this simple rule no longer perfectly preserves board alignment.

Stage 2 — convert desired TCP point into an equivalent “reference-point target”

The main trick is to remove the roll-dependent attachment offsets before solving the arm IK. Using the FK equation from section (2):

\[ P_{TCP} = P_{ref} + d_u \hat{u} + d_v \hat{v} + R\Big(\cos(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{u} + \sin(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{v}\Big) + L_{axis}\,\hat{t} \]

For IK, you want to solve for the arm such that \(P_{TCP}=P^{des}\). If \(q_4\) is chosen, treat the attachment offset as:

\[ \Delta(q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3;q_4) = d_u \hat{u} + d_v \hat{v} + R\Big(\cos(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{u} + \sin(q_4+\phi_0)\hat{v}\Big) + L_{axis}\,\hat{t} \]

Then the arm IK target becomes:

\[ P^{des}_{ref} = P^{des} - \Delta \]

\(\Delta\) depends on \(\hat t,\hat u,\hat v\), and those depend on \((q_0,\gamma)\) (and thus on unknown joint angles). That is why a purely closed-form IK is messy when \(R\neq 0\): roll couples into position.

How to “glue” old IK and the new attachment (practical loop)

You can keep your existing 4‑DOF IK almost unchanged by wrapping it in a short fixed-point iteration:

  1. Start with \(q_0^{(0)}=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\) and \(\gamma=\gamma_{des}\) (often \(\pi\)).
  2. Compute \(q_4^{(0)}\) from your rule (e.g. \(q_4^{(0)}=-q_0^{(0)}+q_{4,0}\)).
  3. Compute \(\hat t,\hat u,\hat v\) and \(\Delta^{(0)}\), then set \(P_{ref}^{des,(0)}=P^{des}-\Delta^{(0)}\).
  4. Run your existing IK on \(P_{ref}^{des,(0)}\) to get \((q_0^{(1)},q_1^{(1)},q_2^{(1)},q_3^{(1)})\).
  5. Update \(q_4^{(1)}\) from the new \(q_0^{(1)}\), recompute \(\Delta^{(1)}\), and repeat 2–3 times.
After this loop, optionally run Newton/LM on the full position residual \(F(q_0..q_3)=P_{TCP}(q_0..q_3,q_4(q_0))-P^{des}\) to refine the solution.

Stage 3 — Newton/LM refinement after the 2–3 iteration \(q_0\) loop

After the fixed-point loop above (which “glues” your old IK to the attachment), you have a good initial guess. Use that guess as the starting point for a short Newton (or LM) refinement on the full TCP position residual.

With \(q_4\) set by your policy (e.g. \(q_4=-q_0+q_{4,0}\)), define a 3-vector residual:

\[ F(q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3)=P_{TCP}(q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3,q_4(q_0)) - P^{des} \]

Clarification: \(P^{des}\) is the constant world position you want to reach (the IK input). \(P_{TCP}(q)\) is the forward-kinematics expression that computes the TCP position from joint angles \(q\). The Jacobian is the matrix of partial derivatives of that FK expression: \[ J(q)=\frac{\partial P_{TCP}}{\partial [q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3]} \] In code you can compute \(J\) either analytically (differentiate the FK equations) or numerically (finite differences).

Then apply a few Newton steps (or LM steps) to reduce \(\|F\|\). In practice you can:

  • Use a numerical Jacobian (finite differences) for \(J=\frac{\partial F}{\partial [q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3]}\).
  • Run 3–10 iterations, stop when \(\|F\|\) is small or when improvement stalls.
  • Enforce joint limits after each update.
This mirrors your current approach with \(dX\): a fast approximate solve produces a good seed, and Newton cleans up the remaining coupled error.
