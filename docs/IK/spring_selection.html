Spring selection for shoulder counterbalance (simplified worksheet)

Goal: reduce shoulder sag/backlash effects by mechanically assisting gravity torque with a spring. This page covers a simplified model and the minimum geometry you must measure to choose a spring.

Rule of thumb: don’t aim for 100% counterbalance on a hobby-servo arm. Start with ~50–80% so the arm still feels stable and doesn’t “float up” aggressively.

1) Simplified setup (the model we are sizing)

  • Only one joint: shoulder.
  • A point mass at the end of the upper arm (at the elbow location in the real robot).
  • Arm “relaxed” pose: pointing up. Arm “loaded/worst” pose: horizontal.
  • Spring is an extension spring with force \(F = F_u + k\,\Delta x\).

Given (your simplified numbers):

  • Mass at end: \(m = 0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\)
  • Upper arm length (shoulder to mass): \(L = 200\,\mathrm{mm} = 0.200\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • Distance between anchor points: about \(100\,\mathrm{mm}\) (one near the shoulder joint, one about halfway along the upper arm)
  • Anchor-to-anchor length increases by \(\Delta x = 10\,\mathrm{mm}\) from “up” to “horizontal”.

2) Step A — compute the gravity torque at horizontal

Worst case is the arm roughly horizontal. Gravity torque about the shoulder is approximately:

\[ \tau_g \approx m g L \]

With \(g \approx 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\):

\[ \tau_g \approx 0.5 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.200 \approx 0.981\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \]

If you prefer kg·cm: \(1\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \approx 10.2\,\mathrm{kg\cdot cm}\), so \(0.981\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\approx 10.0\,\mathrm{kg\cdot cm}\).

3) Step B — you must know the spring’s moment arm about the shoulder

This is the key missing “geometry parameter” needed to pick a spring.

Define \(r_\perp\) as the perpendicular distance from the shoulder axis to the spring’s line of action (at the pose you care about, usually near horizontal).

\[ \tau_{spring} \approx F \, r_\perp \]

The “distance between anchor points is 100 mm” is not automatically \(r_\perp\). Depending on how the spring is routed, \(r_\perp\) can be much smaller than the raw anchor distance. For sizing, measure or estimate \(r_\perp\) directly from a side photo / CAD / ruler measurement.

How to compute \(r_\perp\) (planar, straight-line spring)

Work in the shoulder’s plane (your arm’s vertical plane). Use 2D coordinates \((x,z)\) with the shoulder axis at the origin.

  • Let the arm-side spring anchor point be at distance \(a\) from the shoulder axis along the upper arm.
  • Let \(\theta\) be the shoulder angle measured from “arm up” (\(\theta=0^\circ\)) toward “arm horizontal” (\(\theta=90^\circ\)).
  • Then the arm anchor position is: \[ \mathbf{p}_A(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} a\sin\theta\\ a\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \]
  • Let the base anchor point (fixed in the base frame) be: \[ \mathbf{p}_B = \begin{bmatrix} x_B\\ z_B \end{bmatrix} \]
  • If the spring is a straight line from \(\mathbf{p}_A\) to \(\mathbf{p}_B\), the force direction (unit vector) at the arm anchor is: \[ \hat{\mathbf{f}} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{p}_A}{\|\mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{p}_A\|} \]
  • Then the perpendicular moment arm is the perpendicular distance from the origin to that line of action: \[ r_\perp(\theta) = \left|\det\left(\mathbf{p}_A(\theta),\hat{\mathbf{f}}(\theta)\right)\right| \] (units: mm if you use mm coordinates).
In plain words: \(r_\perp\) is “how far the spring’s pull line passes from the shoulder axis” (measured perpendicular to the line). If the pull line passes through the shoulder axis, then \(r_\perp=0\) and the spring produces no shoulder torque (it only side-loads the joint).

Important: “L-shape over the shoulder axis” usually means \(r_\perp \approx 0\)

If, when the arm is horizontal, the spring physically bends over (or is guided over) the shoulder axis, the segment that pulls on the upper arm is approximately along the line from the arm anchor to the shoulder axis. That line passes through the axis, so the effective moment arm is ~0.

If you use a pulley/roller near the axis, then \(r_\perp\) becomes approximately the pulley radius (often only a few mm), which forces you to use a very strong spring and increases joint side-loads.

Worked example from your described layout (straight-line case)

You described: shoulder axis is ~50 mm above the base; base anchor is ~20 mm behind the axis (\(x_B=-20\,\mathrm{mm}\)) and ~50 mm below it (\(z_B=-50\,\mathrm{mm}\)).

You also said the spring length is ~100 mm when the arm is up. In the “arm up” pose, \(\mathbf{p}_A=[0,a]^T\), so:

\[ 100^2 \approx 20^2 + (a+50)^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad a \approx \sqrt{100^2-20^2}-50 \approx 48\,\mathrm{mm} \]

With that geometry:

  • Arm up (\(\theta=0^\circ\)): \(r_\perp \approx 9.6\,\mathrm{mm}\)
  • Arm horizontal (\(\theta=90^\circ\)): \(r_\perp \approx 28.4\,\mathrm{mm}\)

Why this formula is valid (and how those numbers are computed)

In 2D, the torque magnitude about the origin from a force \(\mathbf{F}=F\hat{\mathbf{f}}\) applied at point \(\mathbf{p}_A\) is:

\[ \tau = \|\mathbf{p}_A \times \mathbf{F}\| = F\,\|\mathbf{p}_A \times \hat{\mathbf{f}}\| \]

The quantity \(\|\mathbf{p}_A \times \hat{\mathbf{f}}\|\) is exactly the “lever arm” (perpendicular distance) because it equals \( \|\mathbf{p}_A\| \sin\phi \), where \(\phi\) is the angle between the radius vector and the force direction. That is why we can define:

\[ r_\perp = \|\mathbf{p}_A \times \hat{\mathbf{f}}\| = \left|\det(\mathbf{p}_A,\hat{\mathbf{f}})\right| \]

With \(\mathbf{p}_A=[x_A,z_A]^T\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{f}}=[f_x,f_z]^T\), the 2D determinant is:

\[ \det(\mathbf{p}_A,\hat{\mathbf{f}})=x_A f_z - z_A f_x \]

Numerical details for your geometry (straight-line spring only)

Use \(\mathbf{p}_B=[-20,-50]^T\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(a\approx 48\,\mathrm{mm}\).

  • Arm up (\(\theta=0^\circ\)): \(\mathbf{p}_A=[0,48]^T\). Then \(\mathbf{p}_B-\mathbf{p}_A=[-20,-98]^T\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{f}} \approx [-0.200,\,-0.980]^T\). So: \[ r_\perp \approx |0\cdot(-0.980) - 48\cdot(-0.200)| \approx 9.6\,\mathrm{mm}. \]
  • Arm horizontal (\(\theta=90^\circ\)): \(\mathbf{p}_A=[48,0]^T\). Then \(\mathbf{p}_B-\mathbf{p}_A=[-68,-50]^T\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{f}} \approx [-0.806,\,-0.592]^T\). So: \[ r_\perp \approx |48\cdot(-0.592) - 0\cdot(-0.806)| \approx 28.4\,\mathrm{mm}. \]
The \(28.4\,\mathrm{mm}\) value is for the straight-line spring (a single line from \(\mathbf{p}_A\) to \(\mathbf{p}_B\)). If your spring is physically routed so it “bends” over the shoulder axis (two segments with a corner at the axis), then the direction of force on the upper arm is along the segment from \(\mathbf{p}_A\) to the axis. That segment passes through the origin, so \(r_\perp \approx 0\) (or \(\approx\) pulley radius if you use a roller).
If you change where the arm-side anchor sits (change \(a\)) or move the base anchor, recompute \(r_\perp\) with the formula above. In practice, increasing \(r_\perp\) (better leverage) is often more effective than buying a stiffer spring.

4) Step C — compute the needed spring force at horizontal

Decide what fraction \(\alpha\) of gravity torque you want to cancel (start with \(\alpha=0.5\) to \(0.8\)):

\[ \tau_{target} = \alpha\,\tau_g \]

Required spring force near horizontal:

\[ F_h \approx \frac{\tau_{target}}{r_\perp} \]

Numerical example (if \(r_\perp \approx 100\,\mathrm{mm} = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\))

  • \(\tau_g \approx 0.981\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • For \(\alpha=0.7\): \(\tau_{target}\approx 0.687\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • \(F_h \approx 0.687/0.10 \approx 6.9\,\mathrm{N}\) (about 0.70 kgf)
If your actual \(r_\perp\) is smaller (e.g. 50 mm), you need roughly 2× the force and roughly 2× the spring stiffness.

5) Step D — pick preload and spring constant \(k\) from \(\Delta x\)

In your simplified model, spring extension changes by \(\Delta x = 10\,\mathrm{mm}\) from “up” to “horizontal”. Model the force as:

\[ F = F_u + k\,\Delta x \]

where:

  • \(F_u\): force in the “up” pose (installed preload / initial tension you still have when the arm is up).
  • \(F_h\): force in the “horizontal” pose.

So:

\[ k \approx \frac{F_h - F_u}{\Delta x} \]

Example numbers (continuing the \(r_\perp=100\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\alpha=0.7\) example)

  • Target \(F_h \approx 6.9\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • Choose a modest preload \(F_u \approx 2\,\mathrm{N}\) (keeps it taut, but doesn’t yank upward too much)
  • \(\Delta x = 10\,\mathrm{mm}\)
  • \(k \approx (6.9 - 2)/10 \approx 0.49\,\mathrm{N/mm}\)
Make sure the spring’s allowed extension/load is not exceeded at maximum reach. If the spring goes near coil-bind or near its max rated extension, it will fatigue quickly or permanently deform.

Example: using an idler/pulley so horizontal \(r_\perp \approx 10\text{–}15\,\mathrm{mm}\)

This is the “spring routed around the shoulder” case where you add a roller/pulley to avoid the line of action passing through the shoulder axis. If the idler geometry only gives you a small effective lever arm (say \(r_\perp \approx 10\text{–}15\,\mathrm{mm}\)) at the horizontal pose, then the spring must generate a much larger force to produce the same counterbalance torque.

Use the same simplified load numbers as above: \(m=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(L=0.200\,\mathrm{m}\Rightarrow \tau_g\approx 0.981\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\). Choose a target compensation fraction \(\alpha\) (start with \(\alpha=0.7\)).

\[ \tau_{target}=\alpha\tau_g \approx 0.7\cdot 0.981 \approx 0.687\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \]

Required spring force at horizontal is:

\[ F_h \approx \frac{\tau_{target}}{r_\perp} \]

  • If \(r_\perp = 10\,\mathrm{mm}=0.010\,\mathrm{m}\): \[ F_h \approx 0.687/0.010 \approx 68.7\,\mathrm{N}\ \ (\approx 7.0\,\mathrm{kgf}) \]
  • If \(r_\perp = 15\,\mathrm{mm}=0.015\,\mathrm{m}\): \[ F_h \approx 0.687/0.015 \approx 45.8\,\mathrm{N}\ \ (\approx 4.7\,\mathrm{kgf}) \]
These forces are large for small brackets/printed parts and will create large side loads and wear. Mechanically, this is why “idler at the axis” (or very close to it) often performs poorly: the lever arm is too small.

Now relate those forces to a spring spec. If the spring extension change between “up” and “horizontal” is still \(\Delta x = 10\,\mathrm{mm}\), and you choose an “up pose” preload \(F_u\), then:

\[ k \approx \frac{F_h - F_u}{\Delta x} \]

Example (pick a moderate preload just to keep tension, \(F_u=10\,\mathrm{N}\)):

  • For \(r_\perp=10\,\mathrm{mm}\): \(k \approx (68.7-10)/10 \approx 5.87\,\mathrm{N/mm}\)
  • For \(r_\perp=15\,\mathrm{mm}\): \(k \approx (45.8-10)/10 \approx 3.58\,\mathrm{N/mm}\)
Practical takeaway: if you can only achieve \(r_\perp\approx 10\text{–}15\,\mathrm{mm}\) at horizontal, you will likely need a spring in the “tens of newtons” force range (multi‑kgf). If that’s too aggressive, the usual fix is not “different \(k\)”, but different geometry: move the idler further off-axis (increase \(r_\perp\)), or use a drum/cable or torsion spring on the shoulder axis.

6) What spring specs to look for (practical shopping list)

  • Spring rate \(k\) (N/mm): how much force increases per mm of stretch.
  • Initial tension (N), if provided: built-in preload for extension springs.
  • Free length \(L_0\) (mm): unloaded length. Installed preload comes from mounting it at a longer distance than \(L_0\).
  • Maximum recommended extension (mm) and/or maximum load (N).
  • End hooks / mounting style: avoid sharp bending; use smooth pins/eyelets to reduce fatigue.
If the math feels fuzzy, prototype with elastic bands and a luggage/fish scale first. Once you know the approximate force needed at “up” and “horizontal”, choosing \(k\) becomes much easier.

7) Minimum parameters required to pick \(k\) (in this simplified model)

From your numbers, \(\Delta x\) alone is not enough. To pick \(k\), you need:

  • \(m\) (kg) and \(L\) (m) to compute \(\tau_g\).
  • \(r_\perp\) (m) near the pose you care about (moment arm of the spring about the shoulder).
  • Target compensation fraction \(\alpha\) (e.g., 0.5–0.8).
  • Desired preload \(F_u\) (N) in the “up” pose.
  • \(\Delta x\) (mm) between “up” and “horizontal” to convert force difference into \(k\).
